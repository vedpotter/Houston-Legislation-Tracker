## What is this?

Senate Bill 10, usually called SB 10, is a Texas law passed in 2025. It requires public elementary and secondary schools to display the Ten Commandments in every classroom. It added a new section, 1.0041, to the Texas Education Code.

The law took effect on September 1, 2025. Since then it has been the subject of three lawsuits, a ruling by the full U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, and a request that the U.S. Supreme Court review the case.

## Why does it matter?

SB 10 applies to every public school in Texas, including open-enrollment charter schools. The Texas Education Agency has said the law has no exceptions for charters or districts of innovation. That includes public schools across the Houston area.

It also matters beyond Texas. Whether a state can require a religious text to be posted in classrooms is a First Amendment question that the Supreme Court last addressed in 1980, in a case about a Kentucky law. Similar laws have been passed or considered in other states.

## What does the law actually say?

- **A display in every classroom.** Each classroom must have a “durable poster or framed copy” of the Ten Commandments in a conspicuous place.
- **Size and legibility.** The display must be at least 16 inches wide and 20 inches tall, and readable by a person with average vision from anywhere in the room.
- **Set wording.** The statute spells out the exact text to be used. The display may include only that text.
- **Donations.** A school that does not have displays for every classroom must accept privately donated posters or framed copies that meet the law's requirements.
- **Purchases.** A school may buy displays but is not required to.
- **Timing.** The law applies beginning with the 2025-26 school year.

## How could this affect students?

- **What students may see.** Students in Texas public schools may see a Ten Commandments poster in their classrooms.
- **What the text of the law does not require.** The statute does not require students to recite, study, or affirm the Ten Commandments. The Fifth Circuit's majority opinion also read the law as authorizing no religious instruction.
- **Where people disagree.** Supporters say a passive display does not pressure anyone. Opponents say a state-selected religious text displayed every day, in a place students are required to attend, can pressure children to conform. Courts are still working out which view the First Amendment supports.
- **What is not yet clear.** Displays were blocked in some districts for part of the 2025-26 school year, and this tracker has not verified how each Houston-area district has handled posting since the April 2026 ruling. The practical effects are not yet clear, and we will update this page as they become clearer.

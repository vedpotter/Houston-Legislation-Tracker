## What is this?

Senate Bill 11, or SB 11, is a Texas law passed in 2025. It lets a school district's board of trustees, or the governing body of an open-enrollment charter school that is not religiously affiliated, adopt a policy that gives students and employees the chance to take part in a period of prayer and reading of the Bible or other religious text on each school day.

The law does not require any school to hold such a period. It does require every board to vote on whether to adopt one.

## Why does it matter?

SB 11 put a religion-in-schools question in front of local school boards across the state, including those in the Houston area. Whether your school has a prayer period depends on how your own board voted, which makes this one of the clearest examples of a state law being carried out one district at a time.

## What does the law actually say?

- **A vote, not a mandate.** Each school board had to take a recorded vote by March 1, 2026, six months after the law took effect, on whether to adopt the resolution described in the law.
- **Consent is required.** A policy must condition participation on a signed consent form. According to the Legislature's bill summary, the form must include an express waiver of the right to bring a claim arising from the adoption of the policy, among other required parts.
- **Privacy for people who do not take part.** The period cannot take place in the physical presence or within hearing of anyone who has not submitted a signed consent form.
- **Not a substitute for class time.** The period cannot replace instructional time.
- **Attorney General support.** According to the bill analysis, the Texas attorney general must, on request, advise districts on compliance, provide a model consent form, and defend a district that is sued over adopting a policy.

## How could this affect students?

- **In districts that adopted a period.** Students could take part with a signed consent form. Reporting indicates the periods are likely to happen outside regular instructional time, such as before school.
- **In districts that did not.** Nothing changes. Students and employees still have existing rights to pray or read religious texts on their own, and several school boards said so when they voted.
- **Questions people have raised.** Opponents worry about peer pressure and about students who do not take part. Supporters say participation is voluntary and protects religious expression. Both views are explained in more detail in our article on the vote.
- **What is not yet clear.** Counts of how many districts adopted the policy differ by source and date. How many periods are actually running, and how they are working in practice, has not been fully documented.

Since the 2025-26 school year, Texas law has required a Ten Commandments poster or framed copy in every public school classroom. The law, Senate Bill 10, has spent much of its short life in federal court. This explainer covers what the law's text requires, what supporters and opponents argue, and where the legal fight stands as of September 20, 2026.

## What does the legislation do?

SB 10 added Section 1.0041 to the Texas Education Code. It requires each public elementary and secondary school to display a durable poster or framed copy of the Ten Commandments in a conspicuous place in every classroom.

The law is specific about the display. It must be at least 16 inches wide and 20 inches tall, and readable from anywhere in the room. The statute spells out the exact wording, and the display may include only that text.

Schools do not have to spend money on the displays, but they may. If a school lacks displays for every classroom, it must accept privately donated ones that meet the law's requirements. The Texas Education Agency has said the law applies to all public schools, with no exception for charter schools or districts of innovation.

The text of the law requires posting. It does not require students to read, recite, or affirm the Ten Commandments.

## Why does it matter to students?

For students in any Texas public school, including those across the Houston area, the most direct effect is what hangs on the classroom wall. How students experience that display is exactly what the lawsuits are about.

Plaintiffs in the lawsuits are Texas families from Christian, Jewish, Hindu, and nonreligious backgrounds. Families from the Houston area are among them: the ACLU of Texas has quoted Rabbi Joshua Fixler of Houston, a parent and plaintiff, in its statements about the case.

The case also matters beyond Texas. The Supreme Court last ruled on classroom Ten Commandments displays in 1980, striking down a Kentucky law in *Stone v. Graham*. A Supreme Court decision on Texas' law could set the rule for the whole country.

## What supporters say

Supporters, including the law's sponsors, argue that the Ten Commandments have long been part of American history and public life. The Senate Research Center's analysis of the bill presents the author's view that displays like this were common for generations, and that a 2022 Supreme Court decision, *Kennedy v. Bremerton School District*, made it legally possible for states to require them.

Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton, whose office defended the law in court, said after the April 2026 ruling that he was proud to defend it and that students should learn from the Ten Commandments. Texas Values, a group that backed the bill, compared the classroom displays to the Ten Commandments monument at the state Capitol.

The Fifth Circuit's majority agreed with the state. It concluded that the law does not create an official religion or force anyone to take part in religious activity.

> “It punishes no one who rejects the Ten Commandments, no matter the reason.”
>
> From the Fifth Circuit majority opinion, as quoted by Houston Public Media

## What opponents say

The families who sued, represented by the ACLU, Americans United for Separation of Church and State, and the Freedom From Religion Foundation, argue that the law violates the First Amendment's Establishment Clause. They point to *Stone v. Graham*, which struck down a similar Kentucky law. They say the state is choosing a particular religious version of the commandments and displaying it to children every day, which pressures them to conform. They also argue that the law interferes with parents' right to guide their children's religious upbringing.

Judge Irma Carrillo Ramirez wrote a dissent, joined by six other judges, saying the law clearly violates the Establishment Clause and the Free Exercise Clause. A commentary published by Canopy Forum, a religion and law publication, argued that students are compelled to attend school and that the Fifth Circuit's reasoning conflicts with both history and recent Supreme Court decisions.

The Fifth Circuit's majority took a different view of *Stone v. Graham*. According to the National Constitution Center, the majority said the Supreme Court's 2022 *Kennedy* decision made that earlier ruling obsolete. The dissenters said only the Supreme Court can overturn its own precedents.

## What has happened so far?

**2025.** The Senate passed SB 10 on March 19, 2025, by a vote of 20 to 11. The House passed it with an amendment on May 25 by 82 to 46, and the Senate agreed to the change on May 28 by 21 to 10. Gov. Greg Abbott signed it on June 21, and it took effect September 1.

**Lawsuits.** Families filed the first lawsuit, *Nathan v. Alamo Heights ISD*, in July 2025. In August, a federal judge in San Antonio blocked displays in the 11 districts named in that case. Other lawsuits followed, including *Cribbs Ringer v. Comal ISD*, and in December 2025 families filed a class action, *Ashby v. Schertz-Cibolo-Universal ISD*, on behalf of districts not already in litigation.

**April 2026.** The full Fifth Circuit ruled 9 to 8 that SB 10 does not violate the First Amendment, reversing the lower-court rulings in *Nathan* and *Cribbs Ringer*. Judge James Ho, who joined the majority, wrote separately that no challenge to laws like this could succeed.

**August 2026.** The families in both cases asked the Supreme Court to review the decision.

## What happens next?

The Supreme Court decides whether to take the case. The side that won below can file a response urging the Court not to hear it. If the Court agrees to hear the case, it could be argued in a future term. We did not find a date for any of these steps in the sources we checked.

Other questions remain open. When we last checked the ACLU of Texas case page, the *Ashby* class action had no ruling. We have also not verified how each Houston-area district has handled displays since the April ruling, and we will follow up on that.

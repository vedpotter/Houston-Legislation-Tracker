Senate Bill 11, passed in 2025, is often described as a Texas school prayer law. That is only partly right. The law does not require any school to hold a prayer period. It requires every school board to take a recorded vote on whether to allow one. The deadline was March 1, 2026, and the results came in district by district.

## What does the legislation do?

SB 11 added Section 25.0823 to the Texas Education Code. It allows the board of a school district, or the governing body of an open-enrollment charter school that is not religiously affiliated, to adopt a policy giving students and employees the opportunity to take part in a period of prayer and reading of the Bible or other religious text on each school day.

The law sets several conditions. Participation requires a signed consent form, and the Legislature's bill summary says the form must include a waiver of the right to bring a claim arising from adopting the policy. The period cannot take place where anyone who has not consented can see or hear it, and it cannot replace instructional time. According to the bill analysis, the attorney general must, on request, advise districts, provide a model consent form, and defend districts that are sued over adopting a policy.

Every board had to hold a recorded vote by six months after the law took effect. That put the deadline at March 1, 2026.

## Why does it matter to students?

Whether a student's school offers a prayer period depends on a decision made by their local school board, often at a public meeting. In districts that adopted a period, students could take part with a signed consent form. Reporting from the Texas Tribune indicates the periods are likely to be held outside instructional hours, such as before school. In districts that did not adopt one, nothing changes.

Students already have some rights to pray or read religious texts on their own during the school day. Several boards pointed to that when they voted against adopting the policy, and some passed resolutions affirming those existing rights.

## What supporters say

Sen. Mayes Middleton, the bill's Senate author, has said that morals and society have declined since the Supreme Court struck down school-sponsored prayer in the 1960s, according to a Nexstar report. The bill analysis describes SB 11 as expanding protections for religious liberty in public schools, following the Supreme Court's 2022 decision in *Kennedy v. Bremerton School District*.

Supporters also stress that the law is voluntary. Rep. David Spiller, the House sponsor, said after the deadline that the law was not a mandate and that boards could revisit their decisions. Faith leaders in the Houston area lobbied boards to adopt the period, as Houston Public Media reported.

## What opponents say

The ACLU of Texas urged school boards to vote no. It argued that religious neutrality in public schools protects the constitutional rights of students, families, and educators. More than 160 faith leaders signed an open letter organized by the Baptist Joint Committee for Religious Liberty, according to Texas AFT. The letter said students who do not take part might be bullied or ostracized, and that some might feel pressure to join to gain favor with teachers or coaches.

Some school board members made a different argument: that the state was putting them in the position of having to vote for or against children's right to pray, when that right already exists.

## What has happened so far?

Counts differ by source and by date. In the days before the deadline, the ACLU of Texas said it knew of at least 100 districts that had declined and 22 that had adopted, and stressed that its numbers were incomplete. After the deadline, the Texas Tribune reported that Rep. Spiller had identified 15 districts that opted in, out of more than 1,200 districts and charter schools.

In the Houston area, Magnolia ISD voted unanimously on February 9 to adopt a resolution and begin drafting local policies, KPRC 2 and KBTX reported. Other districts around the state, such as Killeen ISD, voted not to adopt.

## What happens next?

Boards can revisit their decisions, and Rep. Spiller has said so. We have not found a full, verified count of every district's vote, or a reliable count of how many prayer periods are running. We also did not find a court challenge to SB 11 in the sources we reviewed for this article. If one is filed, we will report it on the [SB 11 tracker page](/legislation/school-prayer-period).
